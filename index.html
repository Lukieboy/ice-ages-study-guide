Study guide · GEOG 205 · Imbrie & Imbrie (1979)

Ice Ages: Solving the Mystery, chapter by chapter

How scientists worked out, over about 140 years, that ice ages happened, that there were many of them, and that they are paced by slow changes in Earth's orbit. Each chapter below names the people, the theories they proposed, and the geological ideas you need to follow the argument.

The book was written by John Imbrie (himself one of the scientists in Chapters 11–15) and Katherine Palmer Imbrie, and finished in June 1978. Everything in the chapter summaries reflects the book. Updates from later research are kept separate, in Reading it in 2026.

The big picture

One mystery, three questions

The book follows the three questions it is organized around. Each one needed new kinds of evidence before it could be answered, and the answers came roughly in this order.

Part I · 1787–1875Did an ice age happen?

Erratic boulders, scratched bedrock and moraines were first blamed on Noah's flood. Swiss naturalists, then Agassiz, argued for vast ancient glaciers. By the 1860s geologists agreed, and by 1875 they had mapped the ice and shown there were several ice ages.

Part II · 1837–1976What caused them?

Of many theories, one could actually be tested: the astronomical theory (Adhemar, Croll, Milankovitch). It rose and fell three times. Deep-sea cores, new dating methods and magnetic reversals finally confirmed it in 1976.

Part III · the futureWhen is the next one?

The orbital theory predicts a slow slide toward full glaciation in about 23,000 years. Carbon dioxide from fossil fuels is likely to interrupt that with a warm "super-interglacial".

Primer

The three orbital cycles

You need these to follow Part II. None of them changes how much sunlight Earth gets in a whole year by more than a tiny amount. What they change is where (latitude) and when (season) the sunlight arrives. The central question of Chapters 5–15 is which season and latitude matter, and whether these small shifts can really drive ice sheets.

CycleWhat changesPeriod (book's figures)Climate effectFirst used by
EccentricityHow stretched the elliptical orbit is. Today ~1%; ranges from near 0 to ~6%~100,000 yrBarely changes yearly total. Controls how strongly precession can affect the seasonsCroll (1864), using Leverrier
Axial tilt (obliquity)Angle of Earth's axis, now 23½°, varying roughly 22°–25°41,000 yrLess tilt means cooler polar summers. Strongest at high latitudesCroll (1875) in principle; Milankovitch calculated it
Precession of the equinoxesWhere on the orbit the solstices fall (is northern summer near or far from the sun?)~22,000 yr (23,000 and 19,000)Shifts seasonal heating between hemispheres. Strongest at low latitudesAdhemar (1842)

Don't confuse two numbers: the axis itself wobbles once every 26,000 years (axial precession, known since Hipparchus). Combined with the slow turning of the orbit, the solstices and equinoxes complete their circuit in about 22,000 years. The 22,000-year figure is the one that matters for climate.

Part I · Ice Age Discovered

Prologue20,000 years ago → 1800s

The Forgotten Ice Age

What did the world look like at the height of the last ice age, and why did people forget it?

The book opens with a picture of Earth 20,000 years ago. In North America, ice spread from centres near Hudson Bay to bury eastern Canada, New England and much of the Midwest under ice averaging more than a mile thick. A second sheet grew from the Canadian Rockies. In Europe, ice spread from Scandinavia and Scotland across Britain, Denmark, northern Germany, Poland and the western USSR, and a separate cap covered the Alps. In all, about 11 million square miles of land that is ice-free today was buried.

So much water was locked up in ice that sea level was 350 feet lower, exposing continental shelves, including a land corridor between Asia and North America. South of the ice lay a belt of treeless tundra, grazed by reindeer and mammoths. In Eurasia this gave way to semi-arid grassland stretching from France to Siberia, while eastern North America had spruce forest.

About 14,000 years ago the ice began to retreat, and within 7,000 years it had shrunk to its present limits (Greenland and some Arctic ice caps). The ice left a scraped and littered landscape: grooved bedrock and jumbled ridges of debris called moraines. Memory of the ice age faded, and by the 1700s geologists explained these deposits as debris from the biblical Flood. Only in the early 1800s did some begin to ask whether water could really move giant boulders hundreds of miles.

Key concepts

Ice sheet
A continent-scale glacier that spreads outward from a centre (here, near Hudson Bay or Scandinavia).
Glacial sea-level fall
Water in the ice comes from the ocean, so big ice sheets mean lower seas (about 350 ft at the peak).
Moraine
A chaotic ridge of rock debris carried by ice and dumped at its margin.

Ideas in place

The Flood (diluvial) explanation
The default view in the 1700s: surface gravels and boulders were left by Noah's flood.

TakeawayThe evidence of glaciation was always visible. The problem was interpretation, and the Flood was the ruling interpretation.

Chapter 11787 – 1837

Louis Agassiz and the Glacial Theory

Who first realized glaciers had once been far larger, and how did the idea reach the scientific world?

The chapter opens on 26 July 1837, with geologists in carriages heading into the Jura mountains near Neuchâtel, Switzerland. Two days earlier, Louis Agassiz, the young president of the Swiss Society of Natural Sciences and already famous for his work on fossil fish, had shocked the meeting. Instead of fish, he talked about the scratched erratic boulders (rocks found far from where they came from) scattered across the Jura, and said they could only be explained by an ancient age of ice.

Agassiz was the last link in a chain. Several people had reached the idea independently and been ignored: the Swiss minister Bernard Kuhn (1787), James Hutton, often called the father of geology (1794), Jens Esmark in Norway (1824), and Reinhard Bernhardi in Germany, who in 1832 proposed a polar ice cap reaching central Germany. The accepted explanation at the time was Charles Lyell's (1833): erratics were carried by icebergs and ice rafts floating in the Flood.

The chain that reached Agassiz began with ordinary mountain people. Jean-Pierre Perraudin, a chamois hunter, noticed scars on hard rocks far below the glaciers and concluded by 1815 that glaciers once filled the whole Val de Bagnes. He told the naturalist Jean de Charpentier, who found the idea "so extravagant" he dismissed it. The highway engineer Ignace Venetz listened instead. After years of caution, in 1829 Venetz argued that immense glaciers had spread from the Alps across the Swiss plain and beyond. The meeting rejected it, but de Charpentier now came round and spent 1829–1833 organizing the evidence. When he presented it at Lucerne in 1834, it was rejected again, and Agassiz was among the doubters. (On his way there de Charpentier met a woodcutter who calmly explained that the Grimsel glacier had once reached Bern.)

In 1836 Agassiz spent the summer at Bex intending to show de Charpentier he was wrong, and was converted within weeks. He then went further than his mentors: a single huge polar ice sheet had covered Europe to the Mediterranean and much of North America. He borrowed the word Eiszeit ("ice age") from the botanist Karl Schimper. This extended version went beyond the evidence and offended de Charpentier. The 1837 "Discourse of Neuchâtel" was hastily written and nearly everyone present rejected it. Élie de Beaumont and Leopold von Buch led the opposition, and Alexander von Humboldt advised Agassiz to go back to fossil fish. But from then on the glacial theory could not be ignored, and Agassiz's Studies on Glaciers (1840) pressed it further.

TakeawayThe theory came from field observation by local people, was systematized by de Charpentier and was publicized, with exaggerations, by Agassiz. Its acceptance depended as much on persuasion as on evidence.

Chapter 21706 – 1865 (last opponent 1905)

The Triumph of the Glacial Theory

Why did the glacial theory meet so much resistance, and how was it won?

The ruling philosophy was catastrophism: Earth's history was a series of epochs, each ended by a catastrophe that destroyed life, and the most recent catastrophe was Noah's Flood. It explained fossils without contradicting the Bible. Examples: in 1706 a mastodon tooth was declared the tooth of a giant drowned in the Flood, and Scheuchzer's "man who witnessed the Flood" turned out (per Cuvier) to be a giant salamander. Agassiz was a catastrophist too. He replaced the flood with ice, but kept the catastrophe.

The key figure to win over was the Reverend William Buckland of Oxford, England's most respected geologist and a committed diluvialist. In 1821 he studied a cave in Yorkshire full of hyena, lion, elephant and hippo bones, decided it was a pre-Flood hyena den, and dated the Flood to 5,000–6,000 years ago (Reliquiae Diluvianae, 1823). Yet Buckland admitted that neither the flood theory nor Lyell's iceberg theory explained everything. Huge erratics, scratched bedrock and the unsorted nature of the "drift" were puzzles, and floating boulders to high mountains would require sea level to rise more than 5,000 feet.

Agassiz showed Buckland the Swiss evidence in 1838 without lasting success. In autumn 1840, after Agassiz's talk at Glasgow and a field trip through Scotland and northern England (with Roderick Murchison, who never converted), Buckland finally accepted the theory. He then showed Lyell moraines two miles from Lyell's father's house, and Lyell "adopted your theory in toto." At the Geological Society of London in November 1840, all three argued for glaciers. The audience was still hostile, and it took about 20 more years for most British geologists to agree.

The authors give four reasons for the slow acceptance:

  • Geologists knew the sea had flooded the land many times, so a marine origin for drift seemed natural.
  • Some drift held seashells ("shelly drift"). James Croll explained this in 1865: ice sheets crossing shallow seas scraped up shells and mud.
  • Few geologists knew glaciers well. Greenland's ice sheet was only established in 1852, Antarctica's size later still. Mountain geologists accepted the idea faster than lowlanders.
  • Agassiz kept exaggerating: ice reaching the Mediterranean (1837), then filling the Amazon valley (1865). Lyell wrote that Agassiz had "gone wild about glaciers".

Agassiz moved to America in 1846, found glacial polish at Halifax the moment he landed, and became a Harvard professor in 1847. Americans such as Timothy Conrad (1839) and Edward Hitchcock (1841) had already applied his ideas. By the mid-1860s the glacial theory was established on both sides of the Atlantic. The last serious attack, Sir Henry Howorth's, came in 1905.

Chapter 31841 – 1875

Exploring the Ice-Age World

Once geologists accepted the ice age, what did they learn about it?

Victorian wealth and national geological surveys (the U.S. Geological Survey was created in 1879) sent geologists into the field. They first studied how living glaciers work. Snow piles up, and once it is thicker than about 100 feet the bottom layers turn to ice that flows downhill, grinding the bed like a file. A glacier's size is set by its snow budget: if more snow falls than melts it grows, otherwise it shrinks until it reaches a new balance. Even a glacier whose edge stays put is always flowing forward. Where melting exceeds snowfall it plasters debris beneath it (lodgement till). When it stagnates and melts in place, it drops ablation till on top, and meltwater carries the rest away as layered outwash. That mix of unsorted till and sorted outwash is why the "drift" had confused Buckland.

With this knowledge they mapped the ice. In North America the terminal moraine runs as a ridge (up to 150 ft high) from Long Island to Washington State, with till to the north and outwash plains to the south (T. C. Chamberlin drew the first map of North America in the ice age, 1891). Striations and erratics traced back to their source showed which way the ice flowed. By 1875 there was a global map. Ice had covered about 17 million square miles, three times today's area. In the northern hemisphere it covered about 13 times today's area, while Antarctica barely grew. They also found that the ice came from several separate centres, such as the Laurentide Ice Sheet centred near Hudson Bay, and not from one polar cap as Agassiz had claimed.

Effects beyond the ice were worked out too:

  • Sea level: Charles Maclaren (1841) realized the ice must have come from the ocean and estimated an 800 ft fall. Charles Whittlesey (1868), using ice thickness of about a mile measured from partly buried mountains like Monadnock, estimated 350–400 ft, close to modern figures. Lower seas created land bridges, including the route by which people first reached North America.
  • Isostasy: Thomas Jamieson (1865) noticed raised marine shells in Scotland and Scandinavia and proposed that the weight of the ice pushed the crust down into a fluid layer below. After the ice melted the sea flooded in at once, then the land slowly rebounded. Land around Lake Superior is still rising about 15 inches per century.
  • Loess: Ferdinand von Richthofen (1870) showed this fine yellow silt was blown by wind from bare outwash plains in front of the ice. It is the basis of the fertile soils of the American Midwest.
  • Wetter West: Howard Stansbury (1852) saw old shorelines above Great Salt Lake. Grove Karl Gilbert (1870s) showed it is the remnant of the vast Lake Bonneville, so the American West was wetter during the ice age (a "pluvial" lake).

The biggest discovery was that there had been more than one ice age. Collomb (1847) found two tills in the Vosges, but the layers between could mean a minor retreat. Archibald Geikie (1863) argued that plant fragments between Scottish tills showed warm intervals. Amos Worthen (early 1870s) found a rich soil buried between tills in Illinois, and Newberry and McGee found a buried forest between two tills. Soils and forests need warm climates, so warm interglacials had separated ice ages.

Part II · Explaining the Ice Ages

Chapter 4Theories from 1830s to 1970s

The Ice-Age Problem

What could make ice sheets grow and then shrink again, and how could any explanation be tested?

This chapter steps out of the chronology to set up the problem. Ice sheets are one part of a climate system of air, sea and ice, all powered by the sun. Incoming and outgoing radiation balance only at about 40°N and 40°S. The tropics gain heat and the poles lose it, so winds and currents (trade winds, hurricanes, the Gulf Stream, the Kuroshio) move heat poleward. Any theory has to respect two linkages. Growing ice takes water from the ocean, so ice volume and sea level move together. Ice also reflects sunlight, so more ice means more cooling and more ice. This radiation-feedback effect (today called ice–albedo feedback) amplifies any small initial change, so most theories focus on finding the initial nudge.

The authors review eight theories and score them:

TheoryMain proponentIdeaVerdict (as of 1978)
Solar variation—The sun's output droppedUntestable: no way to calculate past solar output
Space dust—Dust clouds dimmed (or brightened) the sunUntestable; two versions contradict each other

Score: three rejected and five untestable. The ninth contender, the astronomical theory, is the only one that makes specific, dated predictions, which is why the rest of Part II is about it.

Climate system

The authors judge theories by testability. This is the method behind the whole book.

TakeawayA good theory needs a mechanism and a testable prediction. Feedbacks explain amplification, but something has to start the change.

Chapter 5120 BC · 1754 · 1842 – 1852

Birth of the Astronomical Theory

Could the way Earth moves around the sun cause ice ages?

Astronomical theory: proposed, then refuted

In 1842, five years after Neuchâtel, the Paris mathematics tutor Joseph Adhemar published Revolutions of the Sea, the first suggestion that orbital variations drive ice ages. The chapter explains the astronomy needed:

  • Kepler showed the orbit is an ellipse with the sun at one focus. Earth is closest (perihelion) around 3 January and farthest (aphelion) around 4 July, 3 million miles farther.
  • Seasons come from the 23½° tilt of Earth's axis, not distance. The solstices (21 June, 21 December) and equinoxes (20 March, 22 September) are the "cardinal points".
  • Because Earth moves more slowly near aphelion, northern spring+summer are currently 7 days longer than fall+winter.
  • Hipparchus (about 120 BC) discovered that the axis points in a slowly changing direction. It wobbles like a top once every 26,000 years (axial precession). d'Alembert (1754) explained it as the pull of sun and moon on Earth's equatorial bulge, and showed that combined with the orbit's own rotation, the solstices and equinoxes circle the orbit every ~22,000 years (precession of the equinoxes).

Adhemar's reasoning: the southern hemisphere now has more hours of darkness than daylight each year, so it is cooling, which is why Antarctica has an ice sheet. Every 11,000 years (half a precession cycle) the situation flips, so ice ages alternate between hemispheres. He added a dramatic sideshow: the Antarctic ice would rot into a mushroom shape, collapse, and send an iceberg-laden flood northward.

In 1852 Alexander von Humboldt pointed out the flaw. A hemisphere's temperature depends on the total solar energy received, not hours of darkness. As d'Alembert had shown, a season's loss from being farther from the sun is exactly balanced by the opposite season being closer, so both hemispheres get equal heat each year. Antarctica is cold because of geography: a polar continent isolated from warm currents.

Chapter 61821 – 1890 (theory 1864 – 1875)

The Astronomical Theory of James Croll

Can a better astronomical mechanism be found?

Astronomical theory: revived and taken seriously

James Croll is the underdog of the book. A Scottish farm boy who left school at 13, he taught himself physics and philosophy while working, without much success, as a millwright, carpenter, tea-shop owner, temperance hotelkeeper and insurance salesman. A janitor's job at the Andersonian College in Glasgow (1859) gave him a scientific library, and in 1864 he turned to the cause of the ice ages.

Croll agreed Adhemar was wrong about hours of darkness, but used something Adhemar had ignored. The French astronomer Urbain Leverrier (whose orbital calculations also led to the discovery of Neptune) had shown that the eccentricity of Earth's orbit slowly changes because of the planets' gravitational pull. Today it is about 1%, and it has ranged from near zero to about 6%. Croll calculated eccentricity for the past 3 million years and plotted it, the first geologist to do so. Eccentricity had been high around 100,000 years ago and low for the last 10,000.

His theory, published in the Philosophical Magazine in August 1864:

  • Eccentricity hardly changes yearly heat, but it strongly changes seasonal heating.
  • Winter is the critical season: less winter sunlight lets snow accumulate.
  • Positive feedback: more snow reflects more sunlight and cools further. Croll was the first to develop this idea.
  • Precession decides whether winter falls near or far from the sun, but its effect depends on eccentricity. A circular orbit means no effect.
  • So an ice age happens in a hemisphere when (a) eccentricity is high and (b) that hemisphere's winter solstice falls at aphelion. Ice ages alternate between hemispheres every ~11,000 years during long "Glacial Epochs". The last Glacial Epoch ran from about 250,000 to 80,000 years ago.
  • A second amplifier, ocean currents: colder poles strengthen the trade winds, which shift warm equatorial currents (deflected by the bulge of Brazil) toward the other hemisphere. Croll's explanation of wind-driven currents was essentially correct.

Archibald Geikie hired him at the Geological Survey of Scotland in 1867. In Climate and Time (1875) Croll added that a smaller axial tilt (it varies about 22°–25°) gives the poles less heat. He could not pursue this because Leverrier had not calculated when the tilt changed. Croll became a Fellow of the Royal Society, was injured in 1880, and died in 1890.

Chapter 71864 – c. 1900

Debate Over Croll's Theory

Does the geological record fit Croll's predictions?

Astronomical theory: debated, then abandoned by 1900

James Geikie (Archibald's brother) became Croll's main supporter in The Great Ice Age (1874). His evidence was that drift contained many tills, as many as six in some places, separated by peat with warm-climate plants. A cycle of repeated ice ages was exactly what Croll predicted. T. C. Chamberlin contributed North American evidence of at least three tills.

This chapter also explains where the ice ages sit in the geological time scale. Lyell had divided Earth history into Eras and Periods (1830–1865). In 1846 Edward Forbes proposed using Pleistocene for the time when the glacial and interglacial drift formed. Modern usage: Pleistocene plus Holocene (postglacial time) make up the Quaternary Period of the Cenozoic Era. Geologists also found traces of much older glaciations in Paleozoic and Precambrian rocks (explained in the Epilogue).

There were two ways to test Croll:

  • Did ice ages alternate between hemispheres? No layer could be traced across oceans, so geologists compared how weathered the youngest tills were. Many concluded the hemispheres were glaciated at the same time (Dana agreed). Others said weathering rates are too unreliable to detect an 11,000-year difference. The question was unresolved.
  • Do the dates match? Croll's theory said the last ice age ended ~80,000 years ago. Niagara Falls offered a clock: in 1829 Robert Bakewell Jr. estimated the falls had cut their gorge, receding 3 ft/year, in ~10,000 years. Lyell (1841) revised this to 1 ft/year, or ~30,000 years. Later estimates ranged from 6,000 to 32,000. Newton Winchell's study of St. Anthony Falls gave 8,000. In 1894 James Dana summed up the American position: the glacial period ended 10,000–15,000 years ago, not 80,000.

European geologists, following Geikie (and the astronomer Sir Robert Ball, 1891), mostly stayed loyal. Meteorologists added that Croll's radiation changes were too small to matter. By about 1900 the theory was treated as a historical curiosity. The waterfall dates were right: the last ice sheets really did vanish about 10,000 years ago.

Chapter 81911 – 1958 (theory 1920 – 1941)

Through Distant Worlds and Times

Can the effect of orbital changes on sunlight be calculated precisely?

Astronomical theory: rebuilt on mathematics

Milutin Milankovitch, a Serbian engineer who became professor of applied mathematics at Belgrade in 1909, resolved in a coffeehouse in 1911 to calculate the climates of Earth, Mars and Venus, past and present. Adhemar and Croll had lacked the mathematics to calculate the size of orbital effects. Milankovitch set himself four goals:

  1. Orbital geometry. Already done: Ludwig Pilgrim (1904) had calculated eccentricity, tilt and precession for the past million years (Croll had only eccentricity and precession for 100,000 years).
  2. Sunlight by latitude and season. Using Newton's principles (distance and angle of the sun's rays). He worked through the Balkan War (1912) and World War I, partly as a prisoner of war and then in a Budapest library. His Mathematical Theory of Heat Phenomena Produced by Solar Radiation (1920) showed orbital changes were large enough to cause ice ages.
  3. Past radiation curves. The climatologist Wladimir Köppen (with his son-in-law Alfred Wegener, of continental drift fame) wrote to him. Köppen argued the critical factor is cooler summers, not colder winters as Adhemar and Croll had assumed. Arctic winters are already cold enough for snow, so what matters is whether summer melts it. Milankovitch spent 100 days calculating summer radiation at 55°, 60° and 65°N over the past 650,000 years. Köppen saw the curves matched the Alpine ice-age history of Penck and Brückner, and published them in Köppen & Wegener's Climates of the Geological Past (1924). In 1930 Milankovitch extended the curves to eight latitudes.
  4. Ice-sheet response. Using the snowline (the altitude above which snow lasts all year) he estimated how much ice would grow for a given radiation change, published in 1938. His life's work was summed up in the Canon of Insolation and the Ice-Age Problem, being printed when Germany invaded Yugoslavia on 6 April 1941.

A key result: the shape of the radiation curve depends on latitude. At high latitudes the 41,000-year tilt cycle dominates. Near the equator the 22,000-year precession cycle dominates. Milankovitch died in 1958, before the decisive tests.

Chapter 91880s – 1965

The Milankovitch Controversy

Does the land record confirm Milankovitch's predictions?

Astronomical theory: accepted, then rejected by 1965

The theory's great strength was that it was testable. The 65°N curve had nine radiation minima (ice ages) in an irregular pattern: a triplet at 25,000, 72,000 and 115,000 years ago, three earlier pairs, and one very long warm interval in the middle.

Two land-based records were used to test it:

  • North America: Chamberlin and Frank Leverett recognized four drift sheets: Nebraskan, Kansan, Illinoian, Wisconsin (oldest to youngest). Supporters matched these to Milankovitch's four groups of minima. Critics noted the drifts had no real dates, so the match was inconclusive.
  • The Alps: In the 1880s Albrecht Penck found three terraces above Alpine river valleys. He reasoned that rivers deposit gravel in cold times and cut flat surfaces (straths) in warm times, so each gravel layer marks an ice age. With Eduard Brückner (1909) he named four: Günz, Mindel, Riss, Würm (alphabetical, oldest to youngest). Using lake sediments, they estimated postglacial time at 20,000 years, a 240,000-year "Great Interglacial", and a 650,000-year Pleistocene.

Köppen and Wegener matched Penck–Brückner to the radiation curve in 1924, and the Great Interglacial looked like Milankovitch's long warm interval. Barthel Eberl and Wolfgang Soergel refined the terraces and found an even better match. By the 1930s–40s most European geologists accepted the theory, and Frederick Zeuner used it as a calendar to date the Pleistocene (1946, 1959). The logic had quietly reversed: the theory was being used to date the record instead of the record testing the theory.

Problems built up. Ingo Schaefer found warm-climate snails in "glacial" gravels, and was ignored. Meteorologists said the theory ignored heat transport by air and ocean, and doubted his calculation that ice-age winters were slightly warmer. Then came radiocarbon dating (Willard Libby, 1946–49, available 1951). Cosmic rays make radioactive carbon-14, living things absorb it, and after death it decays at a known rate. It works for material up to about 40,000 years old. Richard Flint and Meyer Rubin showed the Wisconsin drift held several advances, with the last ice maximum 18,000 years ago and rapid melting by 10,000. A 7,000-year lag behind the 25,000-year radiation minimum was acceptable. But a warm-climate peat at Farmdale, Illinois, dated to 25,000 years, sat exactly at a radiation minimum. Goldthwait and Dreimanis's ice-margin record from Indiana to Quebec showed the ice far north at 72,000 years (a predicted minimum) and advances at 60,000, 40,000 and 18,000. By 1965 the theory had lost most of its supporters.

An irony the authors point out: many of these damning chronologies stretched radiocarbon dates beyond the method's reliable range of about 40,000 years.

Chapter 101872 – 1964

The Deep and the Past

Could the sea floor hold a complete record of ice ages?

Astronomical theory: still out of favour

Croll had predicted that the ocean floor would hold a more complete climate record than the land. The HMS Challenger expedition (1872–76, led by C. Wyville Thomson; 50-volume report by John Murray, 1895) found the sea floor blanketed by sediment. Away from the continents there are oozes made of the shells of plankton: foram (calcium carbonate) ooze in warm and temperate seas shallower than ~4,000 m, and opal ooze of radiolaria and diatoms in cold polar waters. Below about 4,000 m shells dissolve, leaving fossil-free brown clay. Crucially, some plankton species live only in warm water and others only in cold, so shifts in species record climate.

Getting long cores was the obstacle. Gravity corers took only about 1 m, and C. S. Piggot's dynamite-driven corer distorted the record. Even so, Wolfgang Schott (1935), studying Meteor expedition cores, counted foram species and found three layers. One warm species, Globorotalia menardii, was absent from the middle layer, which he read as the last ice age. In 1947 Börje Kullenberg invented the piston corer, taking 10–15 m cores. The Swedish Deep-Sea Expedition (Albatross, 1947–48, led by Hans Pettersson) collected them worldwide.

  • Gustaf Arrhenius found calcium-carbonate cycles in Pacific cores, which accumulate very slowly (~1 mm per century). Atlantic cycles ran the opposite way, with faster accumulation (2–3 mm per century).
  • Phleger, Parker and Peirson (1953) found at least nine ice ages in Atlantic cores, but also puzzling layers of shallow-water sand and shells.
  • Heezen and Ewing (1952) solved that: the 1929 Grand Banks earthquake set off a turbidity current (an underwater sediment avalanche) that snapped cables and spread sand far out. Such layers could now be identified and avoided.
  • Maurice Ewing founded Columbia's Lamont Geological Observatory and had ships take a core every day, building the world's largest core library. David Ericson and Goesta Wollin used menardii abundance as a thermometer. Broecker and Kulp radiocarbon-dated the top boundary to 11,000 years, matching a sudden change on land. Ericson lettered the zones from Z (postglacial) downward: Y cold, X warm, W, V (a long warm zone, compared to the Great Interglacial), U.

Ericson's scheme clashed with Cesare Emiliani's isotope results (Chapter 11), even on the same cores. Ericson dated the start of the Pleistocene to the extinction of discoasters (star-shaped fossil plants), which he estimated at 1.5 million years ago. He did not support Milankovitch.

Chapter 111947 – 1969

Pleistocene Temperatures

What exactly does the oxygen-isotope signal in forams measure?

Astronomical theory: minority view

At the University of Chicago, Nobel laureate Harold Urey proposed in 1947 that oxygen isotopes could be a thermometer. Seawater has light oxygen-16 and heavy oxygen-18, and shells grown in colder water take up more oxygen-18. Samuel Epstein and colleagues built the precise lab method (1951). One known catch: the ratio also depends on the isotopic makeup of seawater itself.

Cesare Emiliani, an Italian oil geologist turned graduate student, applied it to planktonic forams in deep-sea cores. His 1955 paper "Pleistocene Temperatures" was a landmark. It found seven glacial–interglacial cycles in 300,000 years, Caribbean surface waters about 6°C colder in ice ages, and a reasonable fit to Milankovitch. He numbered the swings as isotope stages, odd numbers warm and even numbers cold, counting from Stage 1 (today) downward.

Three disputes followed: with Ericson over what the curves meant, with Broecker and Ericson over Emiliani's time scale, and with geologists who called the Milankovitch match a coincidence. A 1965 conference was inconclusive. Ericson argued that ice sheets are rich in light oxygen-16, so growing ice changes seawater and the isotope curve may record ice volume, not temperature. Emiliani disagreed.

John Imbrie (one of the book's authors) and Nilva Kipp developed a statistical multiple-factor (transfer-function) method. It relates today's mix of all 25 planktonic foram species to modern sea-surface temperature and salinity, then applies the equations down a core. Comparing their results with Broecker and van Donk's isotope data for core V12-122 showed Ericson's menardii was responding to something other than temperature (Ericson was wrong), and that Caribbean cooling was only about 2°C, not 6°C (Emiliani was half right). So most of the isotope signal had to be ice volume. At a Paris meeting in 1969, Imbrie spoke to an audience of two, one of whom was Nicholas Shackleton, who had already published (1967) the same conclusion.

Chapter 121956 – 1969

Milankovitch Revival

Can old sea levels be dated well enough to test the theory beyond radiocarbon's range?

Astronomical theory: revived

Rhodes Fairbridge saw 19 evenly spaced beach ridges in southern Australia, suggesting regular sea-level (and so ice-volume) cycles. He had no dates, so the argument was only qualitative. A second dating revolution supplied them: methods based on uranium, thorium, potassium and protactinium. John Barnes and colleagues (Los Alamos, 1956) developed thorium dating for fossil corals up to ~150,000 years old.

Wallace Broecker and David Thurber dated fossil reefs in Eniwetok, Florida and the Bahamas. Sea level had been ~6 m higher about 120,000 years ago, and high again ~80,000 years ago. In 1965 Robley Matthews of Brown University, stuck on Barbados after a wasted trip to Grenada, saw that the island was terraced like a staircase. With Kenneth Mesolella he showed each terrace is a separate fossil reef (the coral Acropora palmata, which grows near the sea surface). Because Barbados is slowly rising, each reef records a former high sea level. Broecker's team dated them at about 82,000, 105,000 and 125,000 years.

The 65°N curve had no peak at 105,000 years, but the curves for lower latitudes (45°N) peaked at all three dates. So the precession cycle mattered more than Milankovitch's 65°N emphasis allowed. Published in 1968 and confirmed in New Guinea and Hawaii, this revived the theory. But three matching dates could still be chance. A longer, well-dated record was needed.

Chapter 131906 – 1971

Signal from the Earth

How can deep-sea cores be dated?

Astronomical theory: the dating tool arrives

In 1906 Bernard Brunhes found that cooling lava records the direction of Earth's magnetic field, and that some lavas point the opposite way. In 1929 Motonori Matuyama concluded the field had reversed during the Pleistocene, and many times before. Most scientists preferred "self-reversal" by unusual minerals. In the late 1950s and early 1960s, work in Russia (Khramov), Iceland (Rutten) and Hawaii (McDougall and Tarling) supported real reversals. In 1963 Allan Cox, Richard Doell and Brent Dalrymple proved it. Using potassium–argon dating (Curtis and Evernden) they showed reversals happened at the same time worldwide, and built a paleomagnetic time scale.

The present normal-polarity interval is the Brunhes Epoch, preceded by the reversed Matuyama Epoch containing short normal "events" (Jaramillo, Olduvai). Two fixed points became vital: the Brunhes–Matuyama boundary at 700,000 years, and the Olduvai event at 1.8 million years.

Ewing and Talwani tried to read reversals in cores in 1956 without clear success. Harrison and Funnell found the Brunhes–Matuyama boundary in Pacific cores in 1964. At Lamont, Neil Opdyke (with Foster and Glass) measured cores chosen by James Hays, the Antarctic radiolarian specialist, and the reversals appeared clearly in all of them, confirming Hays's fossil zones (Hays & Opdyke, 1967). Any core with the magnetic signal could now be dated.

This also settled how long the Pleistocene is. Lyell (1839) had defined it by fossils, Forbes by cold climate, and a 1948 committee by the first cold-water species in southern Italian rocks. Berggren and Hays (1971) showed that event coincides with the Olduvai event, so the Pleistocene began 1.8 million years ago, not 650,000 as Penck and Milankovitch had assumed.

Chapter 141961 – 1970

Pulsebeat of Climate

What is the main rhythm of Pleistocene climate?

Astronomical theory: dated record, but the main cycle is unexplained

In Czechoslovakia, George Kukla and Vojen Ložek (1961) showed that the unglaciated corridor of Central Europe recorded climate in alternating soils (warm, forested times) and loess (cold, dusty polar-desert times). At least ten cycles were exposed near Brno. In 1968 Kukla found five magnetic reversals in the brickyard sequence, giving dates: the main climate cycle was 100,000 years. Each cycle was asymmetric (forest soil, then steppe black soil, then Arctic brown soil, then loess): a long slow cooling and an abrupt warming. He called the sharp boundaries "marklines".

Kukla also showed that Penck's Alpine scheme was wrong. Gravel accumulates in interglacials too. "Würm" gravels contained postglacial logs, Roman bricks, and in one case a bicycle part. The Günz–Mindel–Riss–Würm framework collapsed, and with it Köppen and Wegener's original confirmation of Milankovitch.

Meanwhile Broecker and Jan van Donk (1970), studying Caribbean core V12-122, also found a 100,000-year sawtooth cycle in the isotope record: slow ice growth ended by rapid melting events they called terminations. Kukla's marklines matched the terminations. William Ruddiman and Andrew McIntyre showed the Gulf Stream swung like a gate hinged on Cape Hatteras, flowing toward Britain in interglacials and toward Spain in glacials, eight times in the Brunhes Epoch.

The difficulty was that Milankovitch's theory had not predicted a 100,000-year cycle, since its 65°N curve is dominated by the 41,000-year tilt cycle. Kukla and Mesolella suggested eccentricity works indirectly by setting the strength of the precession effect. They disagreed over whether summer (Mesolella) or winter (Kukla) was critical. The real test would be whether the smaller 41,000- and 22,000-year cycles showed up in the record.

Chapter 151970 – 1976

Pacemaker of the Ice Ages

Are the tilt and precession cycles present in a well-dated climate record?

Astronomical theory: confirmed (1976)

In 1970 James Hays proposed a team effort. CLIMAP began in May 1971 with National Science Foundation funding, linking Lamont, Brown, Oregon State and later others, eventually nearly 100 scientists. One product was a 1976 map of the ice-age Earth 18,000 years ago.

Step 1, a time scale. CLIMAP needed a stratigraphy usable in every ocean. Tsunemasa Saito found Pacific core V28-238 (raised by John Ladd), and Opdyke located the Brunhes–Matuyama boundary 1.2 m down. Shackleton measured its isotopes and divided the Brunhes Epoch into 19 stages. The top 17 matched Emiliani's exactly, and the sequence later extended to 22. His curve from bottom-dwelling forams matched the surface one. Deep water is always near freezing, so the signal had to be ice volume. Since the ocean mixes within about 1,000 years, the stages are globally synchronous. Pinned by radiocarbon at the top and the magnetic reversal at the bottom, the stages could be dated by interpolation. This was the "Rosetta Stone" of late Pleistocene climate (Shackleton and Opdyke, 1973).

Step 2, spectral analysis. Like splitting a chord into its notes, spectral analysis shows which cycle lengths are present. Van den Heuvel (1966) had tried it on Emiliani's data with an outdated time scale. Imbrie and Shackleton's analysis of V28-238 showed a strong 100,000-year peak and weak hints at 40,000 and 20,000. Hays argued that slowly accumulating cores are blurred by burrowing animals (bioturbation), so faster-accumulating cores (more than 2 mm per century) were needed.

Step 3, the right cores. Hays found RC11-120 in the southern Indian Ocean (3 mm per century). Its radiolarian temperature record and isotope ice-volume record showed that northern and southern hemisphere climate changed together, answering Croll's old question against his alternating-hemisphere idea. It only reached 300,000 years, so Hays searched Florida State's Antarctic collection and found E49-18 (Watkins, Eltanin), reaching Stage 13 (~450,000 years). Spliced together, the two gave an undisturbed 450,000-year record.

Step 4, the test. Imbrie used Anandu Vernekar's orbital calculations. The Belgian astronomer André Berger confirmed that precession has two components, 23,000 and 19,000 years. So the prediction was cycles at ~100,000 (eccentricity), 41,000 (tilt), 23,000 and 19,000 (precession). In summer 1974 the spectra showed:

That is what you would expect if the orbit sets the pace and slow ice sheets respond. On 10 December 1976, Hays, Imbrie and Shackleton published "Variations in the Earth's Orbit: Pacemaker of the Ice Ages" in Science, a century after Croll. Still unexplained: why the 100,000-year cycle is so strong.

The Coming Ice Age

When will the present interglacial end?

First, a definition. An interglacial is any time when oak and other broad-leaved trees are widespread in Europe. By that rule the present interglacial, the Holocene, began ~10,000 years ago. The authors compare forecasting methods:

Shorter natural cycles are superimposed. The Little Ice Age (~AD 1450–1850, about 1°C cooler; documented by Hubert Lamb and, for colonial America, David Ludlum) was the latest of five Holocene glacier advances found by George Denton and Wibjörn Karlén, suggesting a ~2,500-year cycle, possibly solar. Combining cycles gives a natural forecast of about 1,000 years of warming, then 22,000 years of cooling.

Then the "unnatural agent". Burning fossil fuels adds carbon dioxide, which acts as a thermal blanket. Mitchell expected the effect to become noticeable by about 2000 and serious in the next century. Burning most fossil fuel reserves could create a "super-interglacial" warmer than anything in a million years, lasting a thousand years or more, melting parts of Greenland and Antarctica and raising sea level. The next ice age would then be delayed, with cooling not beginning for perhaps 2,000 years.

The Last Billion Years of Climate

Why does Earth have ice ages at all, when most of its history was ice-free?

Only three times in the last billion years have the poles held large ice sheets. These long glacial ages each contain many individual ice ages: the late Precambrian (~700 million years ago), the Permo-Carboniferous (~300 million years ago), and the Late Cenozoic, the present one, which began ~10 million years ago in the book's account.

The likely cause of these long regimes is continental drift. Ice builds up when much land sits near a pole. In the Permo-Carboniferous, the supercontinent Pangaea extended over the South Pole, and the glaciated regions (Brazil, Argentina, South Africa, India, Antarctica, Australia) were all at high southern latitudes. As Pangaea moved north, Earth was warmer than today for some 200 million years. About 55 million years ago the Cenozoic climate decline began. Antarctica separated from Australia and settled over the South Pole, and North America and Eurasia moved toward the North Pole. More high-latitude land meant more reflective snow and cooler climate. In the book's sequence, mountain glaciers and a large Antarctic ice sheet appeared ~10 million years ago, Antarctica expanded ~5 million years ago, and northern hemisphere ice sheets appeared ~3 million years ago. Once those northern ice sheets existed, they were sensitive to the orbital cycles, producing the 100,000-, 41,000- and ~22,000-year rhythms.

Beyond the book

Reading it in 2026

The book's history holds up, and its central conclusion (orbital pacing) is now textbook science. Some numbers and forecasts have changed since 1978. These notes come from later research, not from the book. They matter for GEOG 205, especially the course question of whether past natural variations count as evidence against human-caused warming.

Revised numbers

Pleistocene start: moved from 1.8 to 2.58 million years ago in 2009, so the Quaternary now begins with the first major northern glaciations.

Brunhes–Matuyama reversal: now dated to about 780,000 years ago. Ironically the correction came partly from "tuning" sediment records to the orbital cycles, a sign of how well-trusted the theory became.

Last Glacial Maximum: the book's 18,000 years is in radiocarbon years. Calibrated to calendar years it is roughly 21,000 (the peak is often given as about 26,000–19,000 years ago).

Antarctic ice: a large Antarctic ice sheet is now dated to about 34 million years ago (Eocene–Oligocene boundary), well before the ~10 million years in the Epilogue. Northern ice sheets grew markedly about 2.7 million years ago. The late Precambrian glaciation is now often discussed as "Snowball Earth".

Names: Günz–Mindel–Riss–Würm and Nebraskan–Kansan–Illinoian–Wisconsin are no longer used as the global framework. Researchers use Marine Isotope Stages (MIS), descended from Emiliani's numbering.

Still open

The 100,000-year problem, flagged at the end of Chapter 15, is still debated: eccentricity's direct effect on sunlight is tiny. Before about 1 million years ago, ice ages followed the 41,000-year tilt cycle, and the switch (the Mid-Pleistocene Transition) is not fully explained. Ice-sheet dynamics and CO₂ feedbacks are leading candidates.

Carbon dioxide and the forecast

Chapter 4 listed CO₂ as "untestable". Antarctic ice cores later showed CO₂ rising and falling with every glacial cycle over 800,000 years, about 180–190 ppm in glacials and ~280 ppm in interglacials. It acts as a major amplifier of the orbital push, not the trigger.

Mitchell's 1940s–70s cooling is now largely attributed to sulfate aerosol pollution plus natural variability. Warming resumed in the 1970s, roughly on the schedule Mitchell gave for CO₂ (noticeable by about 2000). Atmospheric CO₂ reached about 430 ppm at its 2025 seasonal peak, higher than at any point in the ice-core record.

Because eccentricity is currently low, later orbital models (e.g. Berger & Loutre, 2002) suggest the present interglacial would naturally be unusually long, perhaps another 50,000 years. With today's CO₂, the next glaciation is likely delayed further. The book's 23,000-year natural forecast is now considered too soon.

For the course question: the book's own logic answers it. Orbital cycles operate over tens of thousands of years and currently point toward very slow cooling, so they cannot explain rapid modern warming. The same evidence that proved natural pacing also shows how strongly CO₂ amplifies climate change.
